Flow Matching for World Models

How a velocity field learned by plain regression becomes a simulator, an action labeller and a policy — from the continuity equation to NVIDIA's Cosmos stack. Part II summarises published NVIDIA, GR00T and π0 work; no world model was trained for this project. Every measured number comes from a script in the repository (section 15).

What you will be able to do

  • Derive the conditional flow matching loss and explain why regressing on conflicting straight-line labels learns the right marginal velocity field.
  • Move between velocity, data and noise parameterisations, between flow matching and diffusion (EDM), and between the opposite time conventions used by Meta, Cosmos, π0 and GR00T without sign errors.
  • Explain why one sampling step of an undistilled model returns the conditional mean — the same answer an MSE regressor gives — and what reflow and distillation do about it.
  • Read a frontier world-model stack (Cosmos-Predict2.5, Cosmos 3) and know what each knob does: logit-normal time, resolution shift, guidance, frame conditioning, action injection, token roles, 4-step distillation.
  • Build the whole workflow yourself: curate, pre-train, post-train, critique, distil, evaluate policies and generate synthetic trajectories — with measured results and the failures that taught the most.

Notation used throughout

Unless a section says otherwise, this guide uses the convention of Meta's flow_matching library: \(t=0\) is noise, \(t=1\) is data, \(x_t = t\,x_1 + (1-t)\,x_0\) with \(x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), and the regression target is \(x_1 - x_0\). Cosmos, Stable Diffusion 3 and openpi use the reverse; section 9 has the conversion table. NFE means "number of function evaluations", one forward pass of the network.

Part I

Flow matching from first principles

Nine sections that take you from "a density that moves" to the training and sampling knobs used in production video models.

1 · Generation as transport

An image with \(d\) pixels is a point in \(\mathbb R^d\). Real images occupy a thin, complicated region of that space; call its density \(p_{\text{data}}\). We only ever see samples from it. Generative adversarial networks tried to learn a map to that density in one shot. Diffusion and flow models instead build it gradually: start from a simple density \(p_0\), usually \(\mathcal N(0, I)\), and move it, along a fictitious time \(t\in[0,1]\), until it becomes \(p_1\approx p_{\text{data}}\).

Probability mass is conserved while it moves, so the evolution of the density \(p_t\) and the velocity field \(u_t\) that moves it are tied by the continuity equation of fluid dynamics (Euler, 1757):

\[ \partial_t\, p_t(x) + \nabla\!\cdot\!\big(p_t(x)\,u_t(x)\big) = 0 . \]

Microscopically, each sample follows an ordinary differential equation, \(\dot x_t = u_t(x_t)\). Macroscopically, a whole cloud of samples drawn from \(p_0\) and pushed along that ODE is distributed as \(p_t\) at every time. If we knew \(u_t\), generating data would be easy: draw noise and integrate. Flow matching trains a network \(v_\theta(x,t)\approx u_t(x)\).

This idea is older than flow matching. Continuous normalizing flows (2018) used exactly this ODE, but trained it by maximum likelihood: every training step integrated the ODE backwards from a data point and accumulated the divergence of the field (the instantaneous change-of-variables formula \(\tfrac{d}{dt}\log p_t(x_t) = -\nabla\!\cdot u_t(x_t)\)). That made each step cost dozens of network evaluations, and CNFs never scaled. Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) and rectified flow (Liu et al., 2022) found a simulation-free objective: one network evaluation per training example.

2 · Conditional flow matching

We cannot write down \(u_t\) for the whole data distribution. But we can write down a velocity field that transports noise to a single data point \(x_1\). The simplest one moves every noise sample in a straight line at constant speed:

\[ x_t = t\,x_1 + (1-t)\,x_0, \qquad \frac{dx_t}{dt} = x_1 - x_0 . \]

This is not an arbitrary choice. Between two Gaussians the optimal-transport map is affine, and the optimal way to deform one Gaussian into another is to interpolate linearly between each point and its image (McCann's displacement interpolation, 1997). Shrinking the target Gaussian around \(x_1\) to zero width gives the straight line above. That is why this path is called CondOT (conditional optimal transport).

The training objective is then plain regression onto the constant velocity of the straight line:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb E_{t,\;x_0\sim p_0,\;x_1\sim p_{\text{data}}}\Big\| v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0) \Big\|^2 . \]

Algorithm 1 · one training step of conditional flow matching

  1. Sample a data point \(x_1\) and a noise point \(x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), independently.
  2. Sample a time \(t\) (uniform, or a better distribution — section 7).
  3. Form the point on the straight line, \(x_t = t\,x_1 + (1-t)\,x_0\).
  4. Predict \(v_\theta(x_t,t)\) and regress it onto \(x_1-x_0\) with mean squared error.
  5. Take a gradient step.

With Meta's library the whole loop is a dozen lines:

# pip install flow_matching   (Meta, CC BY-NC 4.0)
import torch
from flow_matching.path import CondOTProbPath
from flow_matching.solver import ODESolver

path = CondOTProbPath()              # x_t = t*x1 + (1-t)*x0, target dx_t = x1 - x0
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
for step in range(8000):
    x1 = sample_data(2048)
    x0 = torch.randn_like(x1)
    s = path.sample(x_0=x0, x_1=x1, t=torch.rand(x1.shape[0]))
    loss = (model(s.x_t, s.t) - s.dx_t).pow(2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

samples = ODESolver(velocity_model=model).sample(
    x_init=torch.randn(5000, 2), step_size=1 / 32, method="euler")

The algorithm looks naive. The noise and data points are paired at random, the label is constant in time although the field we want clearly is not, and the true flow is curved although every training path is straight. The next section shows why none of that is a problem.

3 · Why regression on conflicting labels works

Two training examples can produce the same input \((x_t, t)\) with different labels: two straight lines that cross. The model cannot satisfy both. Mean squared error resolves the conflict in a precise way: for each input it learns the conditional expectation of the label.

\[ v^\star(x,t) = \arg\min_v\, \mathbb E\big[\|v - (x_1-x_0)\|^2 \,\big|\, x_t = x\big] = \mathbb E\big[x_1 - x_0 \,\big|\, x_t = x\big]. \]

That average, weighted by how likely each destination is given that a particle passes through \(x\) at time \(t\), is exactly the marginal velocity field that transports \(p_0\) to \(p_1\):

\[ u_t(x) = \int u_t(x\mid x_1)\,\frac{p_t(x\mid x_1)\,p_{\text{data}}(x_1)}{p_t(x)}\,dx_1, \qquad u_t(x\mid x_1) = \frac{x_1 - x}{1-t}. \]

Equivalently, the conditional loss and the intractable "marginal" loss \(\mathbb E\|v_\theta - u_t\|^2\) differ by a constant that does not depend on \(\theta\), so they have the same gradients. The conflicting labels are the mechanism, not a nuisance.

Two consequences follow. First, the time dependence is real even though each label is constant: the conditional field \((x_1-x)/(1-t)\) points in a fixed direction but grows as time runs out, and a particle on its straight path simply keeps pace. Second, the learned trajectories are curved. Where training paths overlap, the average deflects them; and because an ODE has unique solutions, learned trajectories can never cross. They re-pair noise and data differently from any single training example.

Measured on two moons in this repository (scripts/check_readme_claims.py): straightness is 0.314 for the learned flow and 0.0001 after one round of reflow (section 6).

Key idea

For a data distribution made of a few points \(\mu_i\) with weights \(\pi_i\), the marginal velocity has a closed form: \(v(x,t) = \sum_i w_i(x,t)\,(\mu_i - x)/(1-t)\) with posterior weights \(w_i \propto \pi_i\,\mathcal N(x;\, t\mu_i,\,(1-t)^2)\). The interactive figure in section 5 integrates exactly this field.

4 · Paths, parameterisations, and the link to diffusion

The straight line is one member of the family of affine Gaussian paths, \(x_t = \alpha_t x_1 + \sigma_t x_0\), whose conditional velocity is \(\dot\alpha_t x_1 + \dot\sigma_t x_0\). Meta's library implements these as schedulers:

Velocity, data and noise predictions are interchangeable

Along an affine path, the network can predict the velocity \(u\), the clean data \(\hat x_1\) (x-prediction), or the noise \(\hat\varepsilon\). Each is a linear function of the others and of \(x_t\), so the sampler can always recover a velocity:

\(\hat x_1\) velocity \(u = \frac{\dot\sigma_t}{\sigma_t}x_t + \frac{\dot\alpha_t\sigma_t-\dot\sigma_t\alpha_t}{\sigma_t}\hat x_1\) \(u = (\hat x_1 - x_t)/(1-t)\)
\(\hat\varepsilon\) velocity \(u = \frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}x_t + \frac{\dot\sigma_t\alpha_t-\dot\alpha_t\sigma_t}{\alpha_t}\hat\varepsilon\) \(u = (x_t - \hat\varepsilon)/t\)

\(\hat\varepsilon\) score \(\nabla\log p_t(x) = -\hat\varepsilon/\sigma_t\)

AlphaFlow predicts clean protein structures. In the paper, time runs from a harmonic prior at \(t=0\) to the structure at \(t=1\), and the step is \((\hat x-x_t)/(1-t)\). The public code reverses that clock and steps with \((x_t-\hat x)/t\). Cosmos predicts velocity. After the clock is aligned, the ODE is the same.

Flow matching and diffusion are one family

With Gaussian paths, flow matching and diffusion share the same marginal densities up to a re-parameterisation of time (Meta's ScheduleTransformedModel converts a trained model to a different scheduler without retraining). They differ in four engineering choices: the network's output (velocity, noise or EDM's preconditioned denoiser), the loss weighting over time, the distribution of training times, and the sampler. The mapping to EDM's noise level is exact: in Meta time \(\sigma_{\text{EDM}} = (1-t)/t\), so \(t = 1/(1+\sigma)\).

From the source

Cosmos-Predict2.5 (p. 8): "We adopt flow matching (FM)… While FM and the Elucidated Diffusion Model (EDM)…, which was used in [Cosmos-Predict1]…, are mathematically equivalent in terms of their forward and backward diffusion processes, they differ in how the denoising network is parameterized… FM selects coefficients that make the denoising network predict the velocity of the diffusion trajectory."

5 · Sampling, and the one-step trap

Sampling integrates the learned ODE from noise at \(t=0\) to data at \(t=1\). Euler's method takes \(x_{t+h} = x_t + h\,v_\theta(x_t,t)\), one network evaluation per step; midpoint and Heun use two per step, RK4 four; multistep solvers such as UniPC (used by Cosmos 3) reuse past evaluations. The number of steps you need depends on how curved the learned trajectories are.

Now look at the very first step. With independent noise–data pairs, \(x_0\) carries no information about \(x_1\), so at \(t=0\) the learned velocity is \(v^\star(x_0,0) = \mathbb E[x_1] - x_0\). With a conditioning signal \(c\) (an action, a caption) the same holds conditionally:

\[ x_0 + 1\cdot v^\star(x_0, 0\mid c) = \mathbb E[x_1\mid c]. \]

A single Euler step returns the conditional mean — precisely what a model trained with MSE regression predicts. For a world model that is a blurry average of possible futures, often a physically impossible one. The widget below integrates the exact marginal field for three data points; move the step count and watch the samples collapse onto the mean at one step and split into the three modes as the steps increase.

Measured on two moons: at one Euler step, vanilla flow matching is 13× further from the data than at 64 steps (sliced \(W_2\) 0.626 vs 0.049); after one reflow, one step is already as good as 64.

6 · Straightening, and distillation

Curvature comes from crossings, and crossings come from pairing noise and data independently. Rectified flow (Liu et al., 2022) lets the trained model choose the pairs: draw \(z_0\), integrate the learned ODE to its endpoint \(z_1\), and retrain on the fixed pairs \((z_0,z_1)\). Those pairs come from non-crossing trajectories, so the new conditional paths cross far less and the retrained flow is much straighter; Liu et al. show that repeated reflow drives straightness toward zero, not that one round reaches it. Liu et al. measure straightness as

\[ S = \int_0^1 \mathbb E\,\big\|(Z_1 - Z_0) - v(Z_t,t)\big\|^2\,dt \qquad (S = 0 \text{ means perfectly straight}). \]

z0 = torch.randn(60_000, 2)
z1 = ODESolver(velocity_model=m1).sample(x_init=z0, step_size=1/128, method="midpoint")
# warm-start m2 from m1; train with the same CFM loss on the fixed pairs (z0[i], z1[i])

7 · Training knobs at scale

Where to put the training times

Uniform \(t\) spends capacity on trivially easy and hopeless noise levels. Stable Diffusion 3 and Cosmos-Predict2.5 sample a logit-normal, \(t = \operatorname{sigmoid}(n)\) with \(n\sim\mathcal N(m,s^2)\). Meta's image example has a "skewed timesteps" option that is the same thing biased toward the data end. Cosmos 3 draws the noise level independently for each modality: logit-normal for image, audio and action batches, and "mode sampling" for video.

Intuitively, which outcome happens is settled at high noise and the fine detail at low noise, so the time distribution decides where the model spends its capacity.

The resolution shift

At high resolution, neighbouring pixels are redundant, so a given amount of noise destroys less structure. Cosmos shifts the noise level up with resolution. In the Cosmos convention (\(t=1\) is noise):

\[ t_s = \frac{\beta t}{1+(\beta-1)t} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{t_s}{1-t_s} = \beta\,\frac{t}{1-t}, \]

so the shift multiplies the standard-deviation noise-to-signal ratio \(t/(1-t)\) by \(\beta\). The standard-deviation signal-to-noise ratio is divided by \(\beta\); the variance signal-to-noise ratio \(\alpha^2/\sigma^2\) is divided by \(\beta^2\), which is the \(-2\log\beta\) move in SD3's log-SNR. The 4-step schedule of Cosmos 3's distilled students, t_list = [1.0, 0.9375, 0.8333, 0.625], is exactly this shift with \(\beta = 5\) applied to the uniform grid \([1, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25]\).

One more trick from Predict2.5: drawing 5% of training samples from the highest 2% of the noise distribution sharply reduced abrupt frame transitions. Temporal coherence is decided at high noise.

Loss, masking and weighting

Cosmos uses velocity MSE with no time-dependent weighting. Conditioning tokens (clean context frames, given actions) are masked out of the loss. Cosmos 3 sums per-modality losses with modality-specific scales and multiplies the action loss by 10 "to compensate for the smaller per-element MSE of normalized action vectors."

Classifier-free guidance

Drop the condition about 10% of the time during training; at sampling time, extrapolate from the unconditional toward the conditional velocity:

\[ \tilde v = v_\varnothing + w\,(v_c - v_\varnothing). \]

Guidance doubles the cost per step. Cosmos 3 uses \(w=6\) for video, \(w=3\) for its policy, a two-weight variant for control videos (text 3, control 1.5), and \(w = 1\) (no guidance) for forward and inverse dynamics. Our reading of why: guidance amplifies the condition, and a simulator should reproduce it, not amplify it. Meta's image example writes the same formula as \((1+w')v_c - w'v_\varnothing\), so its default cfg_scale = 0.2 means \(w = 1.2\).

8 · Beyond pixels

Curved spaces: Riemannian flow matching

Rotations, poses and angles live on manifolds. Riemannian flow matching replaces the straight line by a geodesic. In this guide's clock that curve is \(x_t = \exp_{x_0}(t\,\log_{x_0} x_1)\), the same path as Chen and Lipman's \(x_t = \exp_{x_1}((1-t)\,\log_{x_1} x_0)\). Sampling uses a projection step, \(x \leftarrow \operatorname{proj}\big(x + h\,\operatorname{proj}_{T_x}(v)\big)\). Meta's library ships sphere and flat-torus manifolds. Cosmos 3 does not put poses on that manifold solver. Each ego or effector pose is a 9-D relative SE(3) delta: 3-D translation plus a 6-D rotation (the first two columns of the rotation matrix), mapped back to SO(3) by an SVD. Figure 3 of the Cosmos 3 report gives the full vector lengths: an autonomous vehicle is 9D, a single arm is 10D, a humanoid is 29D, and an egocentric human is 57D. A camera or vehicle is the 9D pose alone. A simple gripper adds one dimension. A five-finger hand can be 15D of fingertip positions.

Tokens: discrete flow matching

For tokenised data, each token follows a mixture path \(p_t(x^i\mid x_0,x_1) = (1-\kappa_t)\,\delta_{x_0^i} + \kappa_t\,\delta_{x_1^i}\); the network predicts a distribution over clean tokens, trained with cross-entropy or a generalised KL; sampling is a continuous-time Markov chain that jumps tokens from the source (a mask token or uniform noise; Meta's text example defaults to uniform) toward predicted values. This is the flow-matching view of masked token models, relevant to tokenised world models.

Structure and symmetry: AlphaFlow

AlphaFlow (Jing, Berger, Jaakkola, 2024) turns AlphaFold into a generator of protein conformational ensembles, a learned surrogate for molecular dynamics. Its choices generalise: a structured prior (a Gaussian polymer chain instead of isotropic noise), a symmetry-aware coupling (each noise sample is RMSD-aligned to its data sample, which shortens paths), invariant inputs (the noisy structure enters only as a distance map, through a zero-initialised adapter so training starts at the pretrained model), x-prediction with a geometric loss, and 1-step distillation. On molecular-dynamics ensembles it is judged by distributional statistics — root-mean Wasserstein distance over atoms, RMSF fluctuation profiles, principal-component overlap — not by per-sample error (the paper scores PDB ensembles by lDDT precision, recall and diversity). That is the right way to judge any world model.

Actions: flow-matching policies

Robot policies use the same machinery over action chunks. GR00T N1 (NVIDIA/Isaac-GR00T) and π0 (openpi) both generate action chunks with flow-matching heads; see their papers for time sampling and step counts. In the GR00T talk: "The actions just look like an image to us… like any continuous vectors."

Part II

World models

What a world model is, how the Cosmos stack builds one with flow matching, and how such a model is conditioned, used and judged.

10 · What a world model is

NVIDIA's working definition (Cosmos introduction talk): given the video observed so far \(x_{0:t}\) and a control signal \(c\) — text, a camera pose, an ego trajectory, robot actions, a rendered simulation — predict the future,

\[ p_\theta\big(x_{t+1:T}\,\big|\,x_{0:t},\,c\big). \]

The world model is "the digital twin of the world"; the policy is "the digital twin of the robot". Futures are multimodal — a ball can bounce left or right, a pedestrian can stop or cross — so the model has to be generative. A model trained with mean squared error returns the conditional mean, and the mean of two futures is often impossible.

A ball that can bounce left or right is predicted by a regression model to go straight down. Section 5 shows that one step of an undistilled flow returns that same mean, and section 6 shows reflow fixing it.

Cosmos 3's shared attention

Cosmos 3 runs two parameter towers through every layer. Reasoner tokens (text, images seen through a vision encoder) use causal attention among themselves and never see the noisy tokens. Generator tokens (noisy video, audio and action latents) attend to everything. Because the reasoner side never sees noisy inputs, its keys and values are computed once per sample and reused at every denoising step.

Token layouts make the jobs explicit: text-to-video is \([S_{\text{AR}}, \tilde v_{1:N}, \tilde s]\); video-to-video conditions on \(P\) clean frames, \([S_{\text{AR}}, v_{1:P}, \tilde v_{P+1:N}]\) (a tilde marks noisy tokens). Sampling defaults: 35–50 UniPC steps with guidance 6 and shift 10 for video; guidance 1 for forward and inverse dynamics; 4 steps with guidance 3 for the DROID policy in the paper's evaluation. Headline results for Cosmos3-Super include PAI-Bench-G 80.0 / 82.8 (text / image to video). Physics-IQ image-to-video is 43.8, rising to 48.9 with WMReward best-of-N. Video-to-video is 59.7, rising to 63.4 on the same protocol.

12 · Conditioning a world model

The generative core is generic. What makes it a world model is what it is conditioned on and how.

The action result rewards a moment's thought. Adding the action to the time embedding lets it modulate every layer through adaptive layer norm, the same pathway that tells the network how noisy its input is. Cross-attention is more expressive but makes following the action one more thing the network has to learn to attend to.

13 · One model, many jobs: token roles

Cosmos 3's most useful idea for practitioners: every token is either clean (given) or noisy (to be generated), and the choice of which tokens are noisy defines the task. A third role, dropped, covers absent inputs and classifier-free guidance. Try the presets on an example token layout:

Training mixes the presets per example. Cosmos 3 reports that joint training cut inverse-dynamics error by 72% on PushT. Implementation detail: Meta's ProbPath.sample takes one time per sample, but roles need one time per token (clean tokens at \(t=1\), each modality with its own \(t\)), so the interpolation is applied with the scheduler coefficients directly:

from flow_matching.path.scheduler import CondOTScheduler

def interpolate(x1, x0, t_tok, sched=CondOTScheduler()):
    """x1, x0: (B, N, ...); t_tok: (B, N) one flow time per token."""
    s = sched(t_tok)
    shape = t_tok.shape + (1,) * (x1.ndim - t_tok.ndim)
    a, sg = s.alpha_t.reshape(shape), s.sigma_t.reshape(shape)
    da, dsg = s.d_alpha_t.reshape(shape), s.d_sigma_t.reshape(shape)
    return a * x1 + sg * x0, da * x1 + dsg * x0      # x_t and its velocity target

t = torch.where(role == CLEAN, torch.ones_like(t_sampled), t_sampled)
x_t, target = interpolate(x1, torch.randn_like(x1), t)
loss = ((model(x_t, t, roles) - target) ** 2 * (role == NOISY)).sum() / (role == NOISY).sum()

This function is tested to match CondOTProbPath.sample exactly when all tokens share one time (checked by scripts/check_readme_claims.py).

14 · Using and judging world models

Four ways to use one

  • Evaluate policies. Run each candidate policy closed loop inside the world model and rank them. Cosmos-Surg-dVRK reports a strong correlation between simulated and real success.
  • Plan. Imagine the outcome of candidate action sequences and execute the best first action (model-predictive control). Planning makes latency the bottleneck, which is why distillation matters.
  • Make data. Cosmos-Transfer2.5 augmentation raised a 100-demonstration diffusion policy to 24/30 successes, against 5/30 with standard image augmentation and 1/30 with none.
  • Dream trajectories. DreamGen / GR00T-Dreams: adapt the video world model to a robot, prompt it with new instructions, recover pseudo-actions with a latent-action or inverse-dynamics model, and train a policy on the "neural trajectories".

How to judge one

Axis Metrics
Visual quality VBench dimensions, DOVER, FVD / FID (PAI-Bench-G)
Physics Physics-IQ (motion masks against real outcomes), VideoPhy-2 plausibility
Control fidelity PAI-Bench-C: edge F1, SSIM, depth error, segmentation mIoU
Action following action-conditioned PSNR / FVD; camera pose errors; inverse-dynamics cycle error
Long horizons RNDS (quality relative to ground truth, normalised to the first chunk)
Usefulness success of policies trained or ranked with the model
Distributional fidelity Wasserstein distances to the simulator under identical actions; per-atom \(W_2\) and principal-component overlap (AlphaFlow)

What still breaks

About 10, 15% of the times we generate, we're gonna see a hallucination.
— Sanja Fidler, NVIDIA, Cosmos for autonomous driving (GTC 2025, ~26:06)

We're really thinking of this model as being a renderer. It's not very smart.
— same talk, ~27:09

Traffic lights that are red and green at once, cars that move on red, drift across autoregressive chunks, and latency (the 2025 models were too slow for closed-loop use) are the open problems practitioners name. The remedies in the stack are the ones this guide covers: critics and best-of-N, post-training, distillation, and better conditioning.

16 · Takeaways and checklist

Takeaways

  1. One undistilled step is the regression mean. Measured here: sliced \(W_2\) 0.626 at one step vs 0.049 at 64.
  2. Straighten before you cut steps. One reflow round made a single step as good as 64 (0.046 vs 0.049).
  3. The training loss is not a quality signal. Vanilla loss stays near 1.03 because the labels conflict by design.
  4. Check the clock. Meta, Cosmos, openpi and AlphaFlow disagree on which end is noise (section 9).
  5. Guidance amplifies the condition. Cosmos 3 runs its forward and inverse dynamics at \(w = 1\).
  6. Token roles turn one model into many jobs. Which tokens are noisy selects simulator, labeller or policy (section 13).

Implementation checklist

  • Check the time convention and the sign of the target in every codebase you read (section 9).
  • ODESolver passes a 0-dimensional t to your model; training passes shape (B,) or per-token (B, N). Broadcast inside the model.
  • AffineProbPath's conversion functions expect t already broadcastable to x_t; \(\hat x_1\)→velocity divides by \(\sigma_t\) and is singular at \(t=1\), which Euler and midpoint never evaluate but RK4 does; \(\hat\varepsilon\)→velocity divides by \(\alpha_t\) and is singular at \(t=0\), which every solver evaluates on its first step.
  • Guidance doubles the cost per step; distilled checkpoints already contain it, so run them at guidance 1.
  • The shift is tied to resolution and training stage; re-tune it when the latent size changes.
  • Exact divergence in compute_likelihood only works for flat (B, D) inputs; use the Hutchinson estimator otherwise.

Glossary

CondOT path

The straight-line interpolation \(x_t = t x_1 + (1-t)x_0\); the conditional optimal-transport path between noise and a single data point.

Marginal velocity

The field that moves the whole noise distribution to the data distribution; the posterior average of conditional velocities.

NFE

Number of function evaluations: forward passes of the network during sampling. Guidance doubles it per step.

Reflow

Retraining a flow on its own noise → sample pairs to straighten its paths.

Straightness

\(\int \mathbb E\|(Z_1-Z_0) - v(Z_t,t)\|^2dt\); zero for straight trajectories.

Logit-normal

\(t = \operatorname{sigmoid}(n)\), \(n\sim\mathcal N(m,s^2)\); concentrates training times in the middle (or toward one end if \(m\neq0\)).

Shift β

Re-mapping of the noise level that multiplies the standard-deviation noise-to-signal ratio \(t/(1-t)\) by β, and the variance ratio by β². Larger β is used at higher resolution.

CFG

Classifier-free guidance: \(v_\varnothing + w(v_c - v_\varnothing)\), trained by randomly dropping the condition.

Token role

Clean (given), noisy (generated) or dropped (absent); selects the task of a token-based world model.

Forward dynamics

Predicting future observations from current ones and actions: the simulator.

Inverse dynamics

Recovering the actions that explain an observed trajectory.

Video2World

Continuing a video from a few context frames, optionally given text.

Best-of-N

Sampling N candidates and keeping the one a critic scores highest.

Sliced \(W_2\)

Average one-dimensional Wasserstein-2 distance over random projections; used here to compare samples with data.

rCM, DMD2

Distillation methods used by Cosmos to reach 4 sampling steps.

Plücker rays

A 6-D per-pixel encoding of camera rays used to condition on camera pose.
